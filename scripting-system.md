# Scripting System

A script is not paperwork before the real work. For long-form video, the script *is* the work — it decides whether a viewer stays, learns something, and trusts you enough to watch the next one.

This system gives every video the same reliable structure, with templates for the three formats that cover most educational and founder-led channels.

## Script Structure

Every long-form script follows the same spine:

### 1. Hook — First 30 Seconds

The first 30 seconds decide the video. Your hook must do three things, fast:

- **Name the problem or outcome** in plain language — who is this for, and what is at stake?
- **Create an open loop** — a question, a mistake, a result, or a contradiction the viewer wants resolved.
- **Rule out the wrong viewer politely** — if this video is not for them, let them leave early. Retention from the right audience beats views from the wrong one.

Do not open with:
- A long intro animation or channel history
- "Hey guys, welcome back" filler with no substance
- An agenda list before you have given the viewer a reason to care

Template line:
> "If you [specific situation], [specific costly / painful / confusing thing] is probably happening — and in this video I'll show you exactly how to [fix / avoid / do] it."

### 2. Promise

Immediately after the hook, state the promise explicitly:

- What the viewer will know or be able to do by the end
- Why you are qualified to teach it — briefly, with proof or experience, not a biography
- What the video will *not* cover, if a common misunderstanding exists

Write the promise as one or two sentences in the script. If you cannot write it clearly, the video idea is not ready yet.

### 3. Sections

Break the body into named sections. Each section:

- Opens with the question it answers
- Delivers one idea completely before moving on
- Ends with a bridge to the next section ("That handles X — but it creates Y, which is where most people get stuck")

Practical rules:
- 3–5 main sections for most long-form videos; more than that usually means two videos
- Put the most valuable, most surprising section early — do not save everything for the end
- Write section headings into the script; they become your chapters later

### 4. Pattern Interrupts

Attention decays in a flat delivery. Plan interrupts in the script instead of hoping they happen in the edit:

- A concrete example or short story after an abstract explanation
- A number, document, screen, or visual change
- A direct question to the viewer
- A myth call-out: "Most people think X. It's actually Y."
- A recap line: "So far we've covered A and B. C is where it gets interesting."
- A change of pace — a shorter, punchier passage after a dense one

Mark interrupts in the script with `[INTERRUPT: example / visual / question]` so recording and editing do not miss them.

### 5. CTA — Call to Action

One video, one main CTA. Multiple competing CTAs dilute all of them.

- Place a soft CTA mid-video only if it is genuinely relevant ("I made a checklist for this — it's linked below")
- Place the main CTA at the end, after you have delivered the promise
- Say exactly what to do and why: watch the next video in the series, download the resource, book a call, join the community, or simply subscribe for the follow-up
- Never let the CTA contradict the video ("do it yourself" tutorial ending in a hard sell with no bridge)

## Script Templates

### Template A — Tutorial / How-To

```
HOOK (0:00-0:30)
  The end result, shown or stated. The common mistake that stops people getting it.

PROMISE
  "By the end, you'll be able to [do X] in [scope], without [pain]."

SECTION 1 — What you need before you start
  Prerequisites, tools, accounts, documents. Be specific.

SECTION 2 — Step 1 / Step 2 / Step 3...
  One step per section. Show the result of each step before moving on.
  Call out the exact point where people usually go wrong.

SECTION 3 — Troubleshooting
  The 2-3 most common errors and how to fix each one.

SECTION 4 — What to do next
  The natural next step after this task is done.

CTA
  Next video in the sequence, or the related resource.
```

Best for: step-by-step processes, setup guides, filings, software walkthroughs.

### Template B — Myth-Busting

```
HOOK (0:00-0:30)
  State the myth as believers state it. Hint that it costs people money / time / status.

PROMISE
  "I'll go through [N] myths about [topic], what is actually true, and what to do instead."

MYTH 1 — [statement]
  Why people believe it -> What is actually true -> What to do instead

MYTH 2 — [statement]
  Why people believe it -> What is actually true -> What to do instead

MYTH 3 — [statement]
  Why people believe it -> What is actually true -> What to do instead

SECTION — The pattern underneath
  Why these myths persist, and the one principle that replaces all of them.

CTA
  The deeper tutorial or breakdown that follows from the truth.
```

Best for: misconceptions, outdated advice, "everyone says" topics, compliance and tax myths.

### Template C — Case Breakdown

```
HOOK (0:00-0:30)
  The outcome of the case — good or bad — stated plainly, without spoiling the cause.

PROMISE
  "I'll break down what happened, decision by decision, so you can see where it turned."

SECTION 1 — The starting point
  Who, what situation, what constraints. Only the facts that matter later.

SECTION 2 — The key decisions
  Decision 1: what they did, what happened.
  Decision 2: what they did, what happened.
  Decision 3: the turning point.

SECTION 3 — The result
  Where it ended up, and the numbers / facts that prove it (only verified ones).

SECTION 4 — Lessons you can use
  3 transferable lessons, each tied back to a decision above.

CTA
  Your own next step for the viewer in the same situation.
```

Best for: real client / founder stories (with permission), teardowns, post-mortems, before-and-afters.

> Privacy rule for case breakdowns: anonymise or get permission. Never invent details, figures, or outcomes to make a case sound better.

## Writing the Script

- **Write for the ear, not the eye.** Short sentences. Read every draft aloud — if you stumble, the viewer will too.
- **One idea per sentence, one section per idea group.**
- **Front-load value.** Assume the viewer leaves early; make the first third useful on its own.
- **Script the hook and promise word-for-word.** The body can be tight bullets if you present well, but the opening and the CTA should be exact.
- **Note visuals as you write** — `[SHOW: screen / document / b-roll]` — so recording is not guesswork.
- **Keep a parking lot.** Ideas that do not fit this video go into your idea list, not into this script.

## Batching Scripts Day

Scripting is thinking work. Batch it separately from recording.

A practical batching day:

1. **Pick from the idea list (30–60 min)**
   - Choose the next set of video ideas using your research notes and analytics learnings (see `analytics-loop.md`)
   - For each one, write the promise in a single sentence. If the promise is weak, drop or reshape the idea now.

2. **Outline all of them first**
   - Hook, promise, section headings, CTA — nothing more
   - Review the outlines together: remove duplicates, order any series, check that no two videos make the same promise

3. **Write full scripts, one at a time**
   - Hook and CTA word-for-word; body to your preferred depth
   - Mark pattern interrupts and visual notes as you go

4. **Read-aloud pass**
   - Read each script aloud, fix stumbles, cut anything that sounds written rather than spoken
   - Check the hook again — would *you* keep watching after 30 seconds?

5. **Package as you finish**
   - Draft the title and thumbnail concept while the idea is fresh (see `packaging-checklist.md`) — packaging decided after recording is usually weaker

Then record on a separate day. Scripting day ends with scripts and packaging drafts ready — not with uploads. Publishing still happens one at a time, at human pace, with varied gaps (see `README.md`).
